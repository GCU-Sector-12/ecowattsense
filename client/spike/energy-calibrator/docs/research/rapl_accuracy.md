# RAPL and laptop-battery readings versus wall power: measured accuracy, and how studies and tools turn them into whole-machine energy

> Reading guide (not a section). Every figure is tagged with CPU generation and year. "Snippet-level" means I only saw a search-engine summary of the page or paper and could not open the full text. "Slides" means a non-peer-reviewed presentation. Most hard wall-meter numbers come from servers and workstations; direct studies on office laptops and small desktops are scarce, and where I had to use tool documentation examples or reviews I say so. Notes written 2026-11-11. Windows access paths to RAPL (Energy Meter Interface, drivers) are covered by other researchers; I mention them only where accuracy evidence exists.

## 1. Accuracy of Intel and AMD RAPL (PKG, PP0, PP1, DRAM, PSYS) against external meters, by generation, and known problems

### Takeaway
Intel RAPL was a counter-based model on Sandy/Ivy Bridge (2011-12) and became a voltage-regulator measurement from Haswell (2013-14). On Haswell and later Intel parts with a DRAM domain, package+DRAM follows wall/AC power very closely after a per-machine fit (MAPE 1.7-3.1% on the CSC cluster; residual below 3 W on a 2-socket server), but RAPL never contains the rest of the machine (constant offsets of about 25-50 W on the systems studied), and it is weakest at low power (Alder Lake 2021, DRAM at idle) and on AMD (Zen 2 modelled, package far below AC; 5-43% below IPMI on 2026 server slides). Since Platypus (Nov 2020) Intel can add random noise to RAPL (about 2% at 1 s, 5-15% at 100 ms) and Linux restricts it to root, so averaging windows of 1 s or more are needed.

### Cited Findings

#### Intel by generation (external-meter comparisons)
- Sandy Bridge / Ivy Bridge (2011-12): RAPL is a model, not a physical measurement; it combines architectural event counts with energy weights, and Hackenberg et al. found it depends on workload type. — [Hackenberg et al., ISPASS 2013](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/classes/ece571_2014f/papers/ispass2013-power_measurement.pdf)
- Sandy Bridge-EP, 2-socket Xeon E5 (2012), RAPL package+DRAM vs AC (ZES LMG450 analyzer): RAPL sits well below AC with an offset of about 50 W that is consistent across workloads; accuracy still depends on workload; HyperThreading effects are not reflected. — [Hackenberg 2013](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/classes/ece571_2014f/papers/ispass2013-power_measurement.pdf)
- Sandy Bridge, 1-socket Xeon E3 (2011) vs 12 V P8 DC rail: small deviation for dgemm, memory-bound workloads under-estimated (no DRAM domain on that part), idle slightly over-estimated. — [Hackenberg 2013](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/classes/ece571_2014f/papers/ispass2013-power_measurement.pdf)
- Hackenberg's advice for whole-node energy: the non-CPU consumers (fans, disks, PSU losses) are often small and fairly static, so a reference meter reading can supply the missing part. — [Hackenberg 2013](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/classes/ece571_2014f/papers/ispass2013-power_measurement.pdf)
- Haswell (2013-14): RAPL moved from modelling to measurement through integrated voltage regulators (FIVR). — [Hackenberg et al., 2015](https://tu-dresden.de/zih/forschung/ressourcen/dateien/projekte/firestarter/2015_hackenberg_hppac.pdf?lang=en) and [Platypus paper](https://misc0110.net/web/files/platypus.pdf)
- Haswell-EP, 2 x Xeon E5-2680 v3 (2014-15), package+DRAM (two sockets) vs AC: almost perfect correlation to the AC reference, fitted by a quadratic; remaining deviation of samples from the fit is below 3 W; the reference was a ZES LMG450 (0.07% + 0.23 W); absolute RAPL calibration cannot be confirmed because AC also contains fans, PSU losses and board regulators (the PSU losses are likely non-linear). Sandy Bridge-EP showed a workload bias on the same plot. — [Hackenberg 2015](https://tu-dresden.de/zih/forschung/ressourcen/dateien/projekte/firestarter/2015_hackenberg_hppac.pdf?lang=en)
- Khan et al. (paper 2018; Sandy Bridge-EP and Haswell-EP nodes of the CSC "Taito" cluster, about 1000 nodes, RAPL vs IPMI plug power): predicting plug power from RAPL gave MAPE 1.8% (linear) and 1.7% (GAM) on Haswell with DRAM, 4.3% / 4.0% on Sandy Bridge (no DRAM domain); Haswell without DRAM 3.1%; DRAM alone 7.7%. Using only OS counters (vmstat) the MAPE was 11% / 6% (Sandy Bridge) and 15% / 5% (Haswell). — [Khan et al. 2018, RAPL in Action](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content) (DOI [10.1145/3177754](https://doi.org/10.1145/3177754))
- Same paper, Haswell workstation (Plugwise plug meter, Stream benchmark at several CPU frequencies): correlation coefficient 0.99 between wall power and RAPL package power. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Same paper: RAPL package power correlates with CPU temperature on Haswell (0.93) but much less on a Skylake desktop (0.34), so warm up the CPU before accuracy tests. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Khan et al. 2016 (Plugwise plug meter, Likwid; snippet-level only): wall power is almost perfectly linear in package power, P_wall roughly 1.212 x P_package + 25.58 W on machine 1; the intercept is about the idle wall power (24.65 W and 33.97 W on the two machines); the abstract-level claim is a maximum 5.6% error across processor frequencies. Equations and idle values are from a search-engine summary of the full text, not verified. — [Khan et al. 2016, Comput. Sci. Res. Dev. 31(4)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00450-016-0325-4)
- Hähnel et al. 2012 (Sandy Bridge): RAPL correlates well with a manually instrumented external measurement, with a fixed offset (as summarised by Khan 2018; I could not open the original, HTTP 403). — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Alder Lake desktop, Core i9-12900K (2021; paper 2024; ZES LMG450 AC reference, 0.07% + 0.25 W): consistent correlation between AC and RAPL package overall, with two exceptions. (1) Memory-heavy kernels show lower RAPL package for the same AC power because there is no DRAM domain on this system (it reports 0), so package alone "cannot be used to accurately model total system power". (2) At low power RAPL and AC diverge strongly: RAPL stays near 1.5 W for up to 10 active threads at low frequency while AC power changes and gets noisy; the authors could not explain it. Their summary: RAPL is plausible except at particularly low power. PP0 followed package with a difference of 0.35 W idle, 1.41 W (no memory access) and 2.65 W (with memory access). — [Schöne et al., ICPE 2024](https://research.spec.org/icpe_proceedings/2024/proceedings/p95.pdf)
- Server slides, mixed generations (2026, Glasgow HEPiX, slides, not peer reviewed): RAPL (package; DRAM on Intel) vs IPMI power averaged over 1-minute windows on dual Xeon E5-2630 v4, dual EPYC 7452 (Rome), dual EPYC 7513 (Milan) and single EPYC 7643 (Milan): RAPL energy was 5-43% lower than IPMI; the gap was "not linear nor constant" versus CPU load and hardware dependent; the worst case was the single-socket EPYC 7643 (about 42-43%); the Xeon row was about 17% in the HEP-Score table (row mapping uncertain because the table text extracted jumbled). — [Glasgow RAPL vs IPMI slides, HEPiX Aug 2026](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1708318/contributions/7185021/attachments/3324707/5953607/RAPL_HEPiX_Aug2026_v2.pdf)

#### DRAM, PP0, PP1, PSYS
- DRAM domain, Haswell desktop (i5 with DDR3, 2013-15) and Haswell-EP with DDR4 (Desrochers, Paradis, Weaver, MEMSYS 2016): the overall pattern is good but the result is offset by an amount that varies between systems; the estimate is worse at idle and better under load; integrated-GPU DRAM traffic is not reliably captured; Haswell-EP in "Mode 1" (VR-based measurement) is much better. The project page gives no numeric errors and the paper PDF exceeded my fetch size limit. — [Weaver group DRAM RAPL validation page](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/projects/rapl/rapl_validation.html) (paper: [MEMSYS 2016 PDF](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/projects/rapl/2016_memsys_rapl.pdf))
- DRAM before Haswell was server-only and its values were unstable and differed from references; since Haswell they follow AC reference trends closely. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Haswell-EP: only RAPL DRAM mode 1 is supported; the DRAM energy unit is 15.3 uJ, not the unit in the package unit register; PP0 is not supported on Haswell-EP. — [Hackenberg 2015](https://tu-dresden.de/zih/forschung/ressourcen/dateien/projekte/firestarter/2015_hackenberg_hppac.pdf?lang=en)
- PP0 is the sum of all cores in a package (every core's MSR shows the same value), so per-core energy is impossible on Intel. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- PSYS (Skylake and later, not on all SKUs): Raffin and Trystram plugged ThinkPad L15 Gen1/Gen2 and Alienware m17 R3 laptops into an external wattmeter and found the PSYS domain reported the same consumption as the wattmeter, i.e. the whole laptop (display, dGPU included); they give no error figures. — [Raffin and Trystram, arXiv 2401.15985 (v2, Jul 2024)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)
- PSYS comparisons against battery discharge are in section 3; CodeCarbon docs warn that psys can be unexpectedly high on older Intel (Kaby Lake) and sometimes lower than package on some machines. — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)
- Summing overlapping domains double counts: core and uncore are subsets of package, and CodeCarbon's own example summed 10.88 W for a laptop whose psys was 6.66 W. — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/); [Raffin and Trystram 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)

#### Timing, resolution and counter wrap
- Update interval is about 1 ms with jitter (mean gap 1.00229 ms in one run), values carry no timestamps, and registers update non-atomically (on Haswell PP0 updates 2.8-3.6 us and DRAM 4.4-5.2 us after PKG). — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Because of the missing timestamps, averaging over only 10 ms gives at least 10% inaccuracy (9, 10 or 11 updates may fall in the window), and sampling above about 20 Sa/s cannot keep systematic error below 5%. — [Hackenberg 2013](https://web.eece.maine.edu/~vweaver/classes/ece571_2014f/papers/ispass2013-power_measurement.pdf)
- On Skylake desktop PP0 updates every 50-70 us; Platypus measured PP0 at 50 us and core voltage status at 150 us versus the documented 1 ms. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content); [Platypus](https://misc0110.net/web/files/platypus.pdf)
- Energy units: Sandy Bridge 15.3 uJ; Haswell and Skylake 61 uJ. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content). A mirror of the Intel SDM Vol. 3B gives default energy unit 0EH (61 uJ), a 32-bit counter, update about every 1 ms, and a wrap "around 60 secs" under high power (snippet-level; mirror, not intel.com). — [SDM mirror](https://xem.github.io/minix86/manual/intel-x86-and-64-manual-vol3/o_fe12b1e2a880e0ce-500.html)
- Counters are 32 bit and wrap: a Haswell part at 84 W wraps every 52 minutes (2^32 x 61 uJ / 84 W = 3120 s); Khan suggests sampling at least every 5 minutes for any CPU. — [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Many RAPL tools have had overflow bugs that are hard to see on a mostly idle laptop; perf-events corrects overflow itself, powercap and MSR do not. — [Raffin and Trystram 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)
- Polling too often raises CPU power (and so distorts what is being measured): at 1000 Hz an AMD EPYC server drew about 2.7-3.3 W more; on an Intel server the effect was significant from 10 Hz (powercap, msr) and at 1000 Hz average power rose by 25-49 W depending on the access mechanism, because frequent reads keep an idle CPU out of its deepest C-state (C6). — [Raffin and Trystram 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)

#### Platypus-era changes (2020-2024) that affect RAPL readings
- PLATYPUS (disclosed 2020-11-10) abused unprivileged RAPL access on Intel; CVE-2020-8694 (unprivileged RAPL access, CVSS 5.6) and CVE-2020-8695 (SGX, CVSS 5.3). Linux kernel 5.10-rc4 (commit 949dd0104c49) made energy_uj root-only. — [Intel guidance page](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/software-security-guidance/advisory-guidance/running-average-power-limit-energy-reporting.html)
- Intel microcode "energy filtering": enabled by the ENERGY_FILTERING_ENABLE bit (IA32_MISC_PACKAGE_CTLS MSR 0xBC, bit 0); it can be set but not cleared until processor reset; enabling SGX or TDX can turn it on; BIOS can set it. Filtered values are what RDMSR/MMIO return for package, PP0 and package energy-time status; internal turbo control still uses unfiltered values. Intel warns it "may affect the accuracy of RAPL energy reporting", and notes HWiNFO and CPU-Z may show different readings with SGX/TDX on. — [Intel guidance page](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/software-security-guidance/advisory-guidance/running-average-power-limit-energy-reporting.html)
- Size of the distortion per Intel: original method (IPU 2020.2) could change the power delta between 0 and 50% at 1 s intervals; the IPU 2021.2 method adds random energy noise plus a change of update frequency, with filtered-vs-unfiltered standard deviation typically under 2% at 1 s (table: about 2.1-2.9%) and 5-15% at 100 ms (table: about 4.7-8.2%); IPU 2024.3 scales the noise with package power (larger near TDP) and extends filtering to MSR_PKG_ENERGY_TIME_STATUS (CVE-2024-23984). The update rate of RAPL information stays about every 1 ms. Intel gives no numeric resolution change. — [Intel guidance page](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/software-security-guidance/advisory-guidance/running-average-power-limit-energy-reporting.html)
- Independent check (AWS m5.metal, 2 sockets, idle, 5 minutes, filtering toggled via MSR 0xbc): the filtered signal is very noisy with high variance and a higher mean; no numbers, and the authors say 5 minutes may be too short; they plan to make their tools abort measurement when filtering is active. — [Green Coding Solutions case study](https://green-coding.io/case-studies/rapl-and-sgx)
- Alder Lake i9-12900K: with the filter on, PP0 granularity is 8 ms instead of 1 ms and package energy is reported lower more often, but average power over time did not change significantly. — [Schöne et al. 2024](https://research.spec.org/icpe_proceedings/2024/proceedings/p95.pdf)
- AMD also requires root for its RAPL MSRs (the Linux powercap sysfs interface is Intel-only). — [Platypus](https://misc0110.net/web/files/platypus.pdf)

#### AMD RAPL (Zen)
- Zen 2, dual EPYC 7502 server (paper 2021; ZES LMG670 AC analyzer, accuracy 0.015% + 0.0625 W): RAPL updates every 1 ms. Plotting AC against RAPL package shows no single function, "reminiscent of Intel before Haswell", so AMD's energy data are modelled, not measured. Workloads that do not touch memory still show inconsistent power; memory accesses are not fully captured; there is no DRAM domain; the package domain is "significantly lower" than external measurement, so it cannot be used to estimate total system power. Data-dependent power is not reflected. — [Schöne et al., Zen 2, IEEE CLUSTER 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808)
- Same paper, heavy compute load: RAPL package reported 170 W per processor (TDP 180 W) while the system drew 489 W (one thread per core) or 509 W (two threads per core) at the AC input. — [Schöne 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808)
- AMD slides say its model uses more than 1300 critical-path monitors, 48 on-die supply monitors, 20 thermal diodes and 9 droop detectors (desktop Zen). — [Schöne 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808)
- AMD exposes per-core energy (CORE_ENERGY_STAT) and package energy (PKG_ENERGY_STAT) since Zen (2017). — [Platypus](https://misc0110.net/web/files/platypus.pdf)
- Desktop Threadripper 1950X (2017) in CodeCarbon docs: multi-die package domains can mirror the same socket-wide counter (summing doubles the value); the core domain reports very low values; a single non-mirrored package reading is "generally reliable for total CPU power". — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)
- Server slides above (2026): EPYC Rome/Milan RAPL 5-43% below IPMI energy. — [HEPiX Aug 2026 slides](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1708318/contributions/7185021/attachments/3324707/5953607/RAPL_HEPiX_Aug2026_v2.pdf)
- A "typical accuracy +/-5-10% versus external meters" figure on an HPC course page had no primary source; I excluded it. — [HPC power monitoring course page](https://power-monitoring-e38d4d.pages.code.europa.eu/episodes/PowerMonitoring-1-PowerMonitoringSystems/) (search-result mention only)

#### Windows note relevant to accuracy
- CodeCarbon uses the Energy Meter Interface on Windows 11 and its accuracy page says Windows EMI is "not yet measured against an external reference". — [CodeCarbon methodology](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/methodology/); [CodeCarbon accuracy page](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/accuracy/)
- JoularCore on Windows reads CPU RAPL through EMI (built into Windows 11, no admin rights), the PawnIO driver (admin) or Hubblo's RAPL driver, taking the first that answers. — [JoularCore supported platforms](https://joular.github.io/joularcore/guide/supported_platforms.html)

### Inferences
- For Intel Haswell or newer with a DRAM domain (servers, workstations), a per-machine linear (or quadratic) fit from PKG+DRAM to wall power reaches about 2-6% error on steady loads (1.7-3.1% Khan 2018, 5.6% maximum Khan 2016); this is the best-supported accuracy figure, but it is for servers/workstations and for fits trained on the same machine.
- The dominant error in a "RAPL to wall" estimate is the unmeasured remainder (board, PSU loss, fans, storage, display), not RAPL itself; it was a roughly constant 50 W on a 2-socket Sandy Bridge-EP and about 25-34 W idle wall power on the desktop-class machines in Khan 2016 (snippet-level). For office PCs that idle most of the time, the baseline term will dominate the energy estimate.
- RAPL is least trustworthy exactly where office PCs spend their time (low power): Alder Lake (2021) at low frequency, DRAM at idle (Haswell), and any AMD part. A fleet tool should treat idle RAPL values as a weak signal and rely on per-model idle baselines.
- Wrap time scales with unit/power: 2^32 x 61 uJ is about 262 kJ, i.e. about 4.9 h at 15 W, 67 min at 65 W, 35 min at 125 W, 17 min at 250 W; with the 15.3 uJ unit (Sandy Bridge, Haswell-EP DRAM) it is about 11 min at 100 W. Polling every 1 s (as PowerJoular does) or every few minutes with wrap correction is safe; Windows EMI returns accumulated energy in picowatt-hours with an arbitrary zero point, so power has to be derived by differencing (per Microsoft EMI docs, snippet-level: [EMI overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/windows-hardware/drivers/powermeter/energy-meter-interface)); how it behaves at wrap is not covered in my sources.
- With Intel filtering active (any SGX/TDX-enabled system, or BIOS opt-in) a 1 s window carries about 2% noise and 100 ms windows 5-15%; windows of at least 1 s, ideally 10 s or more, make the filter irrelevant for energy totals, and a tool should detect the filter state (MSR 0xBC bit 0) where it can.
- AMD RAPL needs its own per-family calibration against a wall meter before use; the only external-meter evidence is Zen 2 EPYC servers (2021) and 2026 server slides, nothing for Zen 3/4/5 laptops or desktops.

### Gaps
- No external-meter study with numbers for PSYS (Raffin and Trystram report equality with a wattmeter but without error figures); no quantitative validation of PP1 (graphics) found.
- Numeric DRAM error figures from Desrochers et al. 2016 not obtained (paper file too large for my fetch tool; the project page is qualitative).
- No wall-meter validation of AMD RAPL on Zen 3, 4 or 5 client parts (Ryzen laptops, desktops); Zen evidence is one EPYC 7502 server paper (2021) and slides.
- Khan et al. 2016 equations and the 5.6% maximum error are only from search-engine text of that paper; full text not opened (the open copy I found was a paywalled mirror, so I did not use it).
- Hähnel et al. 2012 (Sandy Bridge) could not be opened (HTTP 403); its numbers are only second-hand.
- The Intel SDM statements (61 uJ unit, 60 s wrap, 1 ms update) come from a third-party SDM mirror; the intel.com page confirms only "about 1 ms".
- No RAPL-versus-wall study of Intel 12th-14th gen or Core Ultra laptops with a calibrated meter; the Alder Lake study used a desktop.

## 2. What share of wall power the processor package makes up (idle and load), and typical idle and load wall power of office desktops and laptops

### Takeaway
The package share is highly platform- and state-dependent: roughly 12-58% of platform power at idle and 52-86% under load on the laptops with published numbers; about 39% at idle and 62% under load on a 2017 HEDT desktop; 53-70% under load on servers. Typical modern business laptops idle at about 2-6 W and draw about 35-65 W under load; modern small business desktops idle at about 10-23 W (manufacturer ENERGY STAR "short idle") and mini PCs reach about 50-55 W under full load; solid wall-measured load figures for full-size office desktops are missing.

### Cited Findings

#### Package (or PSYS) share of platform and wall power
- Core Ultra 7 265H laptop (28 W TDP, 2024-25; powertop battery discharge, psys, package from CodeCarbon docs): idle battery 6 W, psys 6.66 W, package 3.85 W (58% of psys); stress-ng load battery 27 W, psys 24.69 W, package 21.35 W (86% of psys, 79% of battery). — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)
- Core i7-7600U laptop (15 W TDP, Kaby Lake, 2017): idle battery 9.31 W, psys 12.21 W, package only 1.44 W (12% of psys, 15% of battery); stress-ng load psys 29.97 W vs package 15.73 W (52%); the docs flag the 8.40 W battery reading under stress as unreliable and warn about psys anomalies on older Intel. — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)
- Threadripper 1950X desktop (2017) in the same docs: idle (10% CPU) wall 100 W (the page's teaser says 125 W; the accuracy page cites about 115 W from a smart plug) vs RAPL package 38.62 W, about 39% of 100 W; full load wall about 280 W vs package about 172 W, about 62%. The docs are inconsistent on the idle wall figure. — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/); [CodeCarbon accuracy page](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/accuracy/)
- Dual Xeon Gold 6138 server, HPE DL380 Gen10 (2026 slides, external energy meters at the PSU inlets): RAPL-based hardware readings captured only about 53-66% of total system energy. — [Brunnert, HM Munich, SC4RC slides 2026-05-06](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1526482/contributions/7027733/attachments/3268614/5838422/2026-05-06_SC4RC_How_accurate_are_current_tools_and_models_for_software_energy_estimation.pdf)
- Dual EPYC 7502 (Zen 2, 2021) full compute load: two 170 W RAPL packages against 489-509 W AC, about 67-70% (my division of the paper's numbers). — [Schöne 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808)
- Kepler's maintainers expect its RAPL-package CPU values to be 40-70% of IPMI node power (an expectation, not a measurement). — [CNCF Kepler blog 2026-06-30](https://cncf.io/blog/2026/06/30/kepler-re-architected-improved-power-accuracy-and-a-community-call-to-action)
- Jay et al. (2023, DGX-1 with 2 x Xeon E5-2698 v4 Broadwell): regressing the external wattmeter on RAPL-based tools gave a slope of 1.17 (CPU benchmarks) and 1.18 (GPU benchmarks), i.e. wall power is not "RAPL plus a constant"; the relation must be studied per node architecture or per node. — [Jay et al., CCGrid 2023](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CCGRID2023_Ostapenco_Jay_Lefevre.pdf)
- Alder Lake desktop (2021): package RAPL stays at about 1.5 W while up to 10 low-frequency threads run, while the AC input moves independently; absolute AC values are not stated in the text. — [Schöne 2024](https://research.spec.org/icpe_proceedings/2024/proceedings/p95.pdf)

#### Typical wall power: laptops
- Lenovo ThinkPad T14s Gen 3 Intel (Core i5-1235U, 2022), Notebookcheck wall measurement: idle min 2.3 W, average 4.6 W, max 5.6 W; load average 37.1 W, load maximum 65.8 W (the page does not state meter model or adapter). — [Notebookcheck T14s G3 review](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Lenovo-ThinkPad-T14s-G3-Intel-reviewed-quiet-mobile-companion-with-great-performance.676256.0.html)
- ENERGY STAR v9.0 certified data (Core Ultra 7 notebooks, 2025): ThinkPad T14 Gen 6 short idle 4.8 W, long idle 1.2 W, sleep 1.2 W, off 0.2 W, TEC 15.8 kWh/year; ThinkPad T14s Gen 6 short idle 4.1 W, long idle 0.7 W, sleep 0.7 W, off 0.3 W, TEC 11.5 kWh/year. — [ENERGY STAR T14 Gen 6](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4021616); [ENERGY STAR T14s Gen 6](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4016503)

#### Typical wall power: desktops
- ENERGY STAR v9.0 certified HP business desktops (2025), manufacturer-reported certification values (the ENERGY STAR test method was not opened; presumably AC input power): EliteDesk 8 Mini G1i short idle 10.7 W / long idle 4.4 W (TEC 50.6 kWh); ProDesk 2 SFF G1i 14.7 W / 3.0 W (TEC 53.3 kWh); EliteDesk 8 SFF G1i 17.9 W / 3.4 W (TEC 64.2 kWh); EliteDesk 8 Tower G1i 22.6 W / 3.7 W (TEC 78.1 kWh). — [Mini](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/3999662), [ProDesk 2 SFF](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4471356), [EliteDesk 8 SFF](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/3998732), [EliteDesk 8 Tower](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4005857) (values read from each page's PDF export; the page text columns were offset by one row, so mapping follows the field order of the Lenovo records and matches a search-engine summary of the same pages)
- Mini/micro business PCs (ServeTheHome wall measurements, 120 V, mostly used units; snippet-level): ThinkCentre M920q 12-15 W idle, OptiPlex 7080 Micro about 11 W, OptiPlex 7060 Micro with a 65 W CPU just over 18 W, an Alder Lake Micro 5.5-6.5 W. — [ServeTheHome OptiPlex 7060 Micro](https://www.servethehome.com/dell-optiplex-7060-micro-tinyminimicro-at-65w-tdp-cpu-overview/3/)
- Kill A Watt readings for micro PCs (informal, procedure not stated; snippet-level): OptiPlex 5060 Micro (i5-8500T) 15 W idle and 50 W full load; OptiPlex 7050 Micro (i7-7700T) 20 W idle and 55 W full load. — [selfhosting.sh mini PC power table](https://selfhosting.sh/hardware/mini-pc-power-consumption/)
- Older desktops for context (2008-2011 hardware): a Q9550 desktop measured 102 W idle, 125/140/152/165 W at 25/50/75/100% CPU, 4 W asleep; an OptiPlex 790 SFF (i5-2400S) 15 W idle and 51 W under stress. — [Windows team blog 2011](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2011/09/28/measuring-and-reducing-your-pcs-electricity-cost/); [Alphr OptiPlex 790 review](https://www.alphr.com/dell/dell-optiplex-790/31527/dell-optiplex-790-review/) (both snippet-level)

### Inferences
- Package share of wall power is not a constant. Laptops: about 10-60% at idle, 50-85% at load. HEDT desktop: about 40% idle, 60% load. Servers: about 50-70% at load. A fixed "package = X% of wall" scaling would be badly wrong at idle; an affine model (baseline plus slope times package power) matches the data better (slope about 1.2 in Khan 2016 and 1.17-1.18 in Jay 2023).
- For modern office desktops the package at idle is probably only a small fraction (roughly 5-30%) of the 10-23 W ENERGY STAR short-idle wall power: package idle was 1.4-3.9 W on the two laptops, so a 10-20 W small desktop has most of its idle draw in the board, memory, SSD, PSU and fans. This is my arithmetic across different machines, not a measurement on one desktop.
- Typical office fleet picture: laptops about 2-6 W idle and 35-65 W peak load; small desktops about 10-23 W idle and 50-55 W (micro PCs) up to higher values for tower/SFF; display power is excluded from these wall-at-PC figures and is a separate, large term for desktops.
- Because fleet PCs idle most of the time, annual energy is dominated by idle/short-idle watts and by sleep/off policy (ENERGY STAR TEC values 11-78 kWh/year per unit), not by peak load.

### Gaps
- No single study gives package-vs-wall shares at idle and load for a modern office desktop or business laptop measured with a calibrated wall meter; the laptop shares here come from tool documentation examples (battery and psys, not wall) and the only desktop is a 2017 HEDT part.
- No reliable measured load wall power for full-size SFF/tower office desktops (ENERGY STAR gives idle, sleep, off, TEC only; micro-PC load numbers are informal Kill A Watt readings).
- I did not open ENERGY STAR's definitions of "short idle" and "long idle" (presumably display-on versus display-off idle); verify before using them as "idle".
- Notebookcheck's meter model and procedure are not stated on the page I read.
- Monitor/display power is not covered in any of the PC wall figures above.

## 3. Laptop battery fuel gauge accuracy and granularity; studies that use battery discharge as a power reference

### Takeaway
The Windows laptop battery interface (ACPI _BST/_BIX) reports present rate in mW and remaining capacity in mWh, and Microsoft only requires that the reported measurement accuracy is 95% or better, with capacity granularity of at most 1/400 of design capacity in the upper range; sampling and averaging intervals are OEM-defined. The one real DAQ-based characterisation I found (ThinkPad T61 laptop, study of 2010) found 19% RMS error on 0.5 Hz readings that are low-pass filtered with about 16 s delay, falling as readings are averaged. Battery discharge only exists on battery power, excludes the AC adapter and charger losses, and has not been independently validated against wall meters on modern laptops (CodeCarbon docs use it as a rough check: battery discharge was about 10% below to 9% above PSYS on one laptop, and 24% below PSYS on another, where PSYS was 31% higher).

### Cited Findings

#### What Windows requires of the battery interface
- Windows requires ACPI batteries to report in mW and mWh (_BIX power unit must be 0x0), present rate must be above 0 and below 0xFFFFFFFF, and both present rate and remaining capacity "must be accurate within" the Measurement Accuracy field, which "must be set to 95,000 or better" (95% accuracy or better). — [Microsoft Learn, ACPI battery firmware implementation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/acpi-battery-and-power-subsystem-firmware-implementation)
- Capacity granularity: between Warning and Low levels at most 1% of design capacity; between full charge and the warning level at most 75 "milliwatts" (sic; the page says this is about 0.25% of a 25 Wh battery, i.e. 1/400 of design capacity, so the wording is loose). — [Microsoft Learn, ACPI battery firmware implementation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/acpi-battery-and-power-subsystem-firmware-implementation)
- Maximum/minimum sampling time and averaging interval of the fuel gauge are fields in _BIX with "no specific requirement" from Windows; the platform must notify Windows when any _BST data changes, and Windows can avoid polling with a trip point (_BTP). — [Microsoft Learn, ACPI battery firmware implementation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/acpi-battery-and-power-subsystem-firmware-implementation)
- Win32 BATTERY_STATUS: Rate is a signed value in mW (positive charging, negative discharging; arbitrary units per hour if the battery reports relative rates), some batteries report only discharge rates, and the value can be BATTERY_UNKNOWN_RATE; Capacity is in mWh. — [Microsoft Learn, BATTERY_STATUS](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/Power/battery-status-str)
- BATTERY_REPORTING_SCALE returns up to four (granularity, capacity) pairs in mWh because granularity can depend on present capacity. — [Microsoft Learn, BATTERY_REPORTING_SCALE](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/winnt/ns-winnt-battery_reporting_scale)
- Windows' per-app energy estimate (E3, read with `powercfg /srumutil`, in millijoules) only works on devices with a battery and, per Microsoft's author, "doesn't necessarily reflect the energy at the wall" (AC adapter loss, fans, discrete GPU missing). — [Microsoft sustainable-software blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/sustainable-software/measuring-your-application-power-and-carbon-impact-part-1/)

#### Measured behaviour of smart-battery interfaces
- Dong and Zhong characterised battery interfaces against a 1 kHz DAQ on the battery output (system on battery; averaged to a 100 Hz reference): ThinkPad T61 (2007-08, Linux) reads at 0.5 Hz, Dell Latitude D630 at 1 Hz; the T61 gauge returns a low-pass-filtered current (average of ten readings over the last 16 s) with a delay of about 16 s; RMS relative error was 19% at the T61's 0.5 Hz, 33% for a Nokia N85 at 4 Hz and 45% for an N900 at 0.1 Hz; averaging to lower rates reduced error (N85 at 1 Hz about 10%); reading the interface costs little energy. — [Dong and Zhong, Sesame technical report (arXiv 1012.2831, 2010)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.2831)
- Using the smart battery interface only as a low-rate teacher, their self-built model reached 95% accuracy at 1 estimate per second and 88% at one per 10 ms on the T61. — [Sesame report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.2831)
- ThinkWiki notes that the tp_smapi power value is roughly a one-minute average while the ACPI battery file gives the instantaneous draw (snippet-level). — [ThinkWiki, How to measure power consumption](https://www.thinkwiki.org/wiki/How_to_measure_power_consumption)
- Platypus authors mention battery monitoring as another low-resolution software side channel that can identify running applications. — [Platypus paper](https://misc0110.net/web/files/platypus.pdf)

#### Battery discharge used as a reference against RAPL
- CodeCarbon's documentation uses powertop battery discharge as a sanity check for PSYS: Core Ultra 7 265H idle battery 6 W vs psys 6.66 W (psys 11% higher), stress 27 W vs 24.69 W (psys 9% lower); Core i7-7600U idle 9.31 W vs psys 12.21 W (psys 31% higher), stress battery reading 8.40 W flagged "unreliable". Single machines, no external meter. — [CodeCarbon RAPL docs](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)
- Battery discharge is a DC-side measure taken only while on battery (ThinkWiki also warns that running on battery can trigger power-saving modes that change behaviour). — [ThinkWiki](https://www.thinkwiki.org/wiki/How_to_measure_power_consumption)
- TI's fuel-gauge note lists temperature, load, ageing and the battery model as accuracy drivers and says a generic MCU ADC is rarely accurate enough, but it gives no gauge current-accuracy percentages. — [TI application note SSZT786](https://ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SSZT786/GUID-5DD8C3D9-BCBE-467B-90E5-FAA672AAC15D)

### Inferences
- Rate-based readings on Windows are instantaneous-ish, OEM-filtered and only guaranteed to 95%; they are usable for energy over minutes to hours during a discharge, and poor for short events (the T61 example had 16 s lag and 19% RMS error at 0.5 Hz).
- Integrating remaining capacity (mWh) over a discharge interval avoids the rate filter: the stated granularity (at most 75 mWh, or 1/400 of design capacity, in the upper range) means energy changes below about 0.25% of the battery capacity (tens of mWh) are invisible, so intervals should cover at least a few percent of capacity. This is my reasoning from the Microsoft requirement, not a measured result.
- A battery-based power reference cannot cover desktops or laptops on mains power, and on mains the battery shows charging or nothing; so the battery is only useful for calibrating a model per laptop model (like Sesame did) or for on-battery sessions, and it excludes charger/adapter losses that the wall meter sees.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that compares modern (2018+) laptop battery-reported discharge power with a calibrated wall or DC meter; Sesame's numbers are for 2008-2010 hardware, and Microsoft's 95% is a requirement for the declared accuracy, not a measurement.
- Manufacturer gauge datasheet specs (for example TI bq40z50 current and voltage accuracy) were not retrieved.
- Update periods of real Windows laptop gauges (EC polling intervals) are OEM-specific and not documented in the sources found.
- The "battery as ground truth" use in CodeCarbon docs is anecdotal (one or two laptops, powertop, Linux).

## 4. Methods that combine a processor reading with a model to estimate whole-machine energy, and their reported error against wall meters

### Takeaway
Reported RAPL-only tools capture about half to two thirds of server wall energy (53-66%) and CPU-load-times-TDP fallbacks deviate from RAPL by about -60% to +190%; no tool documentation reports a wattmeter validation on office PCs. The better-supported methods add a per-machine calibrated baseline and slope to a RAPL (or PSYS) reading (1.7-5.6% error on servers/workstations), use platform power (IPMI/Redfish) as the node total, or use SPECpower-trained utilization models, which are described as under-estimating and are not published with error numbers.

### Cited Findings

#### Studies that map a processor reading to wall power
- Linear/GAM regression of plug power on RAPL: MAPE 1.7-1.8% Haswell (with DRAM), 3.1% without DRAM, 4.0-4.3% Sandy Bridge; DRAM alone 7.7%; OS counters alone 5-15%. — [Khan et al. 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)
- Linear wall-power model from RAPL package power with a maximum 5.6% error across frequencies (snippet-level). — [Khan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00450-016-0325-4)
- RAPL-based tools (Energy Scope, PowerAPI, Scaphandre, Perf) correlate at about 0.95 with an external wattmeter on a Broadwell DGX-1 (2023), always report less than the wall meter (the omitted parts are fans, storage, PSU, network), the regression slope of wattmeter on tool is 1.17-1.18, and CodeCarbon 2.0.0 (the version tested) reported almost twice the energy of the other RAPL-based tools (the paper tested Scaphandre 0.4.1 and PowerAPI/SmartWatts of Feb 2022). — [Jay et al., CCGrid 2023](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CCGRID2023_Ostapenco_Jay_Lefevre.pdf)
- Classical full-system model comparison (Rivoire et al., HotPower 2008; servers, a mobile fileserver and a 2005 Turion laptop): accuracy defined as under 10% mean error; even a simple linear CPU-utilization model met it on averaged benchmarks, but CPU utilization fails when the CPU is not the dominant dynamic consumer (some laptops, disk-heavy), for non-CPU-bound workloads, and with multicore/power-managed CPUs; performance-counter models were the most accurate and portable. Per-platform numbers are only in figures. — [Rivoire et al. 2008](https://static.usenix.org/event/hotpower08/tech/full_papers/rivoire/rivoire_html)
- Brunnert (2026 slides, HPE DL380 Gen10, dual Xeon Gold 6138, external meters at both PSUs): RAPL-based hardware readings 53-66% of external energy; tool outputs on the slides' scale where external measurement = 100% (my reading of the scale): container/process level about 16-65%, transaction level about 45-64%; Kepler (v0.11.2) and Scaphandre (v1.0.2) significantly under-estimate at low CPU utilization, so idle power must be distributed; the formula-based OpenTelemetry Java agent extension had accuracy comparable to the RAPL-based tools. — [Brunnert slides 2026](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1526482/contributions/7027733/attachments/3268614/5838422/2026-05-06_SC4RC_How_accurate_are_current_tools_and_models_for_software_energy_estimation.pdf)

#### Tool by tool
- CodeCarbon (machine mode): CPU from RAPL package by default (psys only with rapl_prefer_psys=True); on Windows 11 via EMI; without counters it uses CPU TDP times a load factor (machine mode 0.1 + 0.9 x (load/100)^3, process mode linear; threads x 4 W if the model is unknown; 85 W without psutil; a flat 50% of TDP when psutil is missing); RAM 5 W per DIMM (10 W minimum on x86); GPU only via NVML/AMDSMI with no fallback; it does not model disks, network, display, cooling; the page calls the shapes "plausible defaults, not measurements". — [CodeCarbon methodology](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/methodology/)
- CodeCarbon's own validation page: "no external wattmeter validation of any backend"; its load-times-TDP estimate vs RAPL (stress-ng sweeps, January 2025) deviated +40% (dual Xeon E5-2620 v3), +37% (Xeon E3-1240 v2), +3% (Threadripper 1950X, range -57% to +18%), +88% (EPYC 8024P, range -50% to +88%); the overall machine-mode range is about -60% to +190%; the TDP fallback "gets the order of magnitude right". — [CodeCarbon accuracy page](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/accuracy/)
- Independent test of CodeCarbon 3.0.1 on an i9-13900K + RTX 4090 workstation against a LogiLink plug meter (2025, GPU-dominated): dynamic estimates followed the patterns but consistently under-estimated by 20-30%; static TDP estimates were off by -40 to +40%; absolute error often over 100 W; maximum observed power was 534 W. CPU-only inference showed larger errors. — [Fischer, arXiv 2509.22092](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22092)
- Scaphandre: reads RAPL through powercap and attributes it to processes/containers by CPU-usage share; in the 2023 comparison its per-process estimate covered CPU only (PowerAPI also did DRAM) and it sampled at 0.5 Hz in the tested setup; the 2026 slides show it under-estimating energy at low utilization; a GitHub user found it lower than both IPMI and a Shelly Plug S, as expected since RAPL omits PSU loss and disks (anecdotal; snippet-level). — [Jay et al. 2023](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CCGRID2023_Ostapenco_Jay_Lefevre.pdf); [Brunnert slides 2026](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1526482/contributions/7027733/attachments/3268614/5838422/2026-05-06_SC4RC_How_accurate_are_current_tools_and_models_for_software_energy_estimation.pdf); [Scaphandre discussion 338](https://github.com/hubblo-org/scaphandre/discussions/338)
- Kepler (v0.7.2 with Redfish/iDRAC platform power, Dell R640 with 2 x Xeon Gold 6226R, 2025): node power from the BMC with RAPL-derived components; whole-node RMSE versus iDRAC was 66.4 W, mostly reporting latency, but total energy error was under 1%; container-level attribution was not trustworthy (power attributed to idle or non-running containers); newer versions had random wrong-value bugs. Kepler splits power into idle and dynamic parts (ratio model) and shares idle by size. — [Pijnacker et al., arXiv 2504.10702 (2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.10702v1.pdf)
- Kepler rewrite (2026): reads RAPL package from /proc and /sys without eBPF, drops idle attribution for now, keeps a trained-model fallback for VMs without counters; the validation shown is qualitative (IPMI as shape reference); no MAPE/RMSE published. — [CNCF Kepler blog 2026-06-30](https://cncf.io/blog/2026/06/30/kepler-re-architected-improved-power-accuracy-and-a-community-call-to-action)
- PowerJoular / JoularCore: CPU power from RAPL package (Linux powercap; Windows via EMI/PawnIO/Hubblo driver; macOS powermetrics); wrap corrected if read before half the range (every 1 s); process power = CPU power x process CPU share; total = sum of supported components (CPU + GPU); single-board computers use polynomial regression models measured per board; no wattmeter error figures published. — [PowerJoular how it works](https://joular.github.io/powerjoular/ref/how_it_works.html); [JoularCore supported platforms](https://joular.github.io/joularcore/guide/supported_platforms.html)
- Green Metrics Tool: besides RAPL providers it has a "PSU Energy AC XGBoost" provider that estimates whole-machine AC power from CPU utilization plus machine parameters (CPU chips, frequency, threads, TDP, memory GB; optional cores, year, vhost ratio) with an XGBoost model trained on SPECPower_ssj2008 data (the "Cloud Energy" model); docs give no error bounds. The authors' blog says the XGBoost model "tends to under-estimate", the linear model fits badly, and an SDIA formula cannot represent idle and mis-estimates 1-chip and 2-chip machines; the slides say "we are underestimating" with no falsification and RAPL extrapolation of unmeasured machines. No percentages in the sources I could read. — [GMT PSU XGBoost provider](https://docs.green-coding.io/docs/measuring/metric-providers/psu-energy-xgboost-machine); [GMT blog](https://www.green-coding.io/blog/specpower-model-with-xgboost-open-sourced/); [GMT PyData slides](https://www.green-coding.io/slides/PyData-Talk.pdf)
- Cloud Carbon Footprint: average watts = min + average vCPU utilization x (max - min), constants per micro-architecture from SPECPower (fallback averages AWS 0.74/3.5 W, GCP 0.71/4.26 W, Azure 0.78/3.76 W per vCPU), utilization defaults to 50% when unknown, PUE 1.1-1.185, memory 0.000392 kWh per GB-hour, storage and network coefficients; it states point estimates without confidence intervals and "we cannot guarantee their accuracy"; no formal validation is cited. — [CCF methodology](https://www.cloudcarbonfootprint.org/docs/methodology/)
- Sesame (2010-11): learns a high-rate whole-system model from battery-interface readings (see section 3). — [Sesame report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.2831)

### Inferences
- The tools split into three families: (a) RAPL/PSYS sum (Scaphandre, PowerJoular, CodeCarbon default, new Kepler): systematically low versus wall, 30-50% on servers; (b) RAPL or utilization plus a platform total from IPMI/Redfish/wall (old Kepler with Redfish, Hackenberg-style calibration): total energy error under 1-6%; (c) SPECpower-trained utilization models (CCF, GMT XGBoost): no wattmeter validation published, GMT admits under-estimation, and their training data are servers, not PCs.
- The approach with the best published evidence for a fleet tool is an affine calibration per hardware model: P_wall is about P_idle(model) + k x (P_pkg - P_pkg,idle) (or from PSYS where PSYS is validated), with P_idle, k fitted on a few reference machines per model against a calibrated meter. Expect a few percent on steady loads from the server literature; for laptops and AMD no published error exists, so a validation sample per model family is mandatory.
- Treat utilization x TDP fallbacks as order-of-magnitude only (CodeCarbon's own page shows -57% to +187% against RAPL), and never sum RAPL domains.
- Because wall energy also includes display and adapter losses, the tool should report "PC energy excluding display" and state the scope.

### Gaps
- No published wall-meter validation for Scaphandre, PowerJoular/JoularCore on Windows, Green Metrics Tool RAPL providers, or Cloud Carbon Footprint; for CodeCarbon only a 2025 workstation study and Jay 2023 (older version).
- The GMT XGBoost error percentages (in-sample/out-of-sample SPECPower) were not readable in the slides text; the ICPE 2024 per-job power study of XGBoost found in search (under 10% error) is not verified.
- Kepler accuracy for VMs and bare metal client PCs: not found; Kepler is Kubernetes-centred and not a PC tool.
- Brunnert's per-tool percentages are in figures I could not read; only the ranges on the summary slides were readable.
- No source found for how the Windows E3 model is calibrated or its accuracy.

## 5. Smart-plug accuracy (Shelly, Tasmota, TP-Link) at low loads, for validation

### Takeaway
There is no controlled, multi-brand low-load benchmark of Shelly, Tasmota-flashed and TP-Link plugs. Chip datasheets promise 0.2-0.5% over 1000:1-2500:1 ranges, but real plugs are limited by shunt resolution, calibration, firmware thresholds and dynamic loads: Tasmota documents invalid readings below about 5 W, reviewers saw Shelly Plug S report nothing below about 2 W and about 11% error at low load (Gen3, one review), and the only peer-reviewed study (2023) found 54-100% energy error for four of five plug brands on fast-varying power-electronics loads (a PC power supply is such a load). Validate each plug model against a reference meter at your PC's idle and load levels.

### Cited Findings
- e-Prime 2023 (Santos, Duggan, Davis, Zimmerle; abstract-level via search): five plugs (Belkin, TP-Link, Etekcity, Emporia, Sonoff) tested with loads switching from 0.01 to 2 Hz and steady loads; the Belkin plug's maximum energy error never exceeded 1.7%, the other four had maximum energy errors of 54-100%; the authors say the large load-dependent errors call into question using such devices for research data acquisition, especially for highly variable power-electronic loads. Full text not opened. — [DOAJ record, e-Prime 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.prime.2023.100137](https://doaj.org/article/d4bb2be5645c4a28895ab01e491f4b5b)
- Tasmota documentation: some metering chips (HLW8032 in Blitzwolf SHP5, CSE7766 in Sonoff S31/POW R2) "occasionally report invalid power measurements for load values below 5W"; by default 128 invalid readings are needed before reporting no load, and SetOption39 1 makes loads under 6 W read as no load; calibrate with a resistive, power-factor-1 load and a calibrated meter, and not with switch-mode devices such as LED lamps or computer equipment. — [Tasmota power-monitoring calibration](https://tasmota.github.io/docs/Power-Monitoring-Calibration)
- Chip claims (datasheet-level, snippet-level): BL0937 active-power error 0.3% typical, 0.5% maximum, within a 2500:1 input range (4 mA to 30 A with a 1 mOhm shunt); HLW8012 0.2% over 1000:1. Neither figure gives watts of resolution in a finished plug. — [BL0937 listing](https://lcsc.com/product-detail/Energy-Metering-ICs_BL-Shanghai-Belling-BL0937_C94616.html); [HLW8012 datasheet](https://wmsc.lcsc.com/wmsc/upload/file/pdf/v2/lcsc/HLW8012_C83804.pdf)
- Shelly: the manufacturer documentation I reached gives no accuracy or minimum-load figures for Plug S; a 12/2022 home&smart test saw no consumption below 2 W on Shelly Plus Plug S; a Gen3 review comparing against an ATORCH S3 meter reported about 11% deviation at low load (single reviewer; I did not open the page); a community Powercalc profile lists a standby draw of 0.74 W for the plug itself. — [home&smart Shelly Plus Plug S test](https://www.homeandsmart.de/shelly-plug-s-test); [techtest.org Plug S Gen3 review](https://techtest.org/shelly-plug-s-gen3-review-die-smarte-steckdose-ohne-cloud-zwang-ideal-fuer-balkonkraftwerke/); [Powercalc Shelly profile](https://library.powercalc.nl/profiles/shelly/snpl-00112eu) (all snippet-level)
- TP-Link Kasa / Tapo: only forum and review anecdotes (a KP125M reported down to about 0.5 W; another user found Emporia the only plug of three that detected loads under 1 W; Tapo "about as accurate as calibrated Tasmota"; review-site claims of within 2-4% unsourced). — [Home Assistant forum thread on low-power plugs](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/list-of-smart-plugs-that-can-detect-low-5w-power/1026874); [Home Assistant forum, sub-1 W plugs](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/power-monitoring-smart-plug-accurate-under-1w/757154) (anecdotal)
- A smart-socket design paper calibrated against a Yokogawa WT310 reference reports that accuracy can degrade to over 1% in low power ranges and that calibration errors matter most at low power (snippet-level). — [Analysis of Smart Power Sockets Implementation](https://www.academia.edu/61562153/Analysis_of_Smart_Power_Sockets_Implementation)
- Professional reference instruments used in the RAPL literature: ZES LMG450 (0.07% + 0.23-0.25 W) and LMG670 (0.015% + 0.0625 W); Plugwise plug meters were used as the reference in Khan 2016/2018, and a LogiLink plug in Fischer 2025. — [Hackenberg 2015](https://tu-dresden.de/zih/forschung/ressourcen/dateien/projekte/firestarter/2015_hackenberg_hppac.pdf?lang=en); [Schöne 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808); [Khan 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content); [Fischer 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22092)

### Inferences
- A PC at idle (laptop 2-6 W, small desktop 10-23 W) sits right where cheap plugs are weakest (under 5-10 W) and the load is a switch-mode, fast-varying power-electronics load; plugs should be qualified per model against a lab meter at roughly 2, 5, 10, 25, 50 W with a real PC load before they are used as ground truth.
- Plug readings are "wall-side" and include PSU/adapter losses and standby draw of the charger, so they are the right scope for whole-machine validation; their own consumption (about 0.5-1 W) is a further offset at low loads.
- Prefer integrated energy (Wh) comparisons over long windows to instantaneous watt readings, and avoid Tasmota calibrations done with LED or computer loads.

### Gaps
- No controlled published benchmark of Shelly Plug S/PM, Tasmota-flashed plugs and TP-Link Kasa/Tapo against a reference analyzer at 0.5-10 W.
- e-Prime 2023 full text (methodology, load types, firmware versions) not read; the ScienceDirect/DOAJ pages were not accessible.
- Shelly and TP-Link manufacturer accuracy specifications at low load not found; the 11% and 2 W claims are single-review, snippet-level.
- Whether PC-type loads (power factor about 0.5-0.9 with harmonics) change the plug error is not documented in the sources found.
