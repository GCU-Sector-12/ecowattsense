# You can measure the processor and the battery, not the wall socket

An ordinary program, without administrator rights and without its own driver, can today measure only two things on Windows: **the processor package energy on Windows 11** (Intel and AMD, through the Energy Meter Interface, EMI) and **the laptop battery charge on Windows 10 and 11**. On ordinary computers nothing except an external meter can measure the energy from the wall socket. On Windows 10 there is no path for the processor: on the HP 250 G7 laptop the "Energy Meter" counter set has no instances, and the power from Intel Dynamic Tuning answers only an administrator (own measurement, 11 Nov 2026). It is very likely that an ordinary user can open EMI on Windows 11, because Firefox, Chromium and CodeCarbon open it from a process without raised rights. But nobody has published the access rights of this device, and we did not check it on Windows 11. The battery measures the whole laptop, but only when it runs on the battery: the night of 10-11 November cost **32.7 Wh (average 2.28 W)**, and the `energy_rates` watt table would give **about 50 Wh, which is 53% more** (own measurement). The processor package is from about 12% to about 86% of the computer's power draw, and there is no published validation of EMI, of the package on laptops, or of AMD processors against a meter at the wall socket. So the EcoWattSense client must not write these readings into `energy_uj`: it holds the energy from the wall socket, and the server marks every value it receives as "measured". The client should send the readings in separate fields, and they should be used to calibrate the table separately for each computer model.

*Labels. "Own measurement, 11 Nov 2026" means results from the HP 250 G7 laptop (Intel Core i5-8265U, Windows 10 Pro 22H2, Intel Dynamic Tuning 8.7.10201.13396). Some details come from the raw probe files and from the `energy-log.csv` log, not only from the summary. "Research emd-1" is a separate study of the Intel "Energy Meter" driver. "Own calculation" is a calculation by the author of the report on data from the notes. The sources in the notes are strong (Microsoft documentation, program code), indirect (several independent programs) or weak (one small project, search engine summary). I mark the weak ones in the text.*

## Windows 11 exposes RAPL through EMI without a driver, Windows 10 does not

EMI is a driver interface that Microsoft described for Windows 10. The client finds the devices with `SetupDiEnumDeviceInterfaces` (GUID `{45BD8344-7ED6-49cf-A440-C276C933B053}`), opens them with `CreateFile` and calls four IOCTLs: `IOCTL_EMI_GET_VERSION`, `IOCTL_EMI_GET_METADATA_SIZE`, `IOCTL_EMI_GET_METADATA` and `IOCTL_EMI_GET_MEASUREMENT` ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/powermeter/energy-meter-interface)). A measurement is the accumulated energy in **picowatt-hours (pWh)** and the time in units of 100 ns. The zero point is arbitrary, so the power is computed from the difference of two readings ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/emi/ns-emi-emi_channel_measurement_data)). The units give the formula **W = 0.036 × ΔpWh / Δticks** and **1 pWh = 3.6 nJ** (own calculation). Chromium converts it the same way ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/components/power_metrics/energy_metrics_provider_win.cc)).

Microsoft does not describe who can open the EMI device. We know only that `IOCTL_EMI_GET_MEASUREMENT` needs only `FILE_READ_ACCESS` (code `0x0022400C`) ([NtDoc](https://ntdoc.m417z.com/ioctl_emi_get_measurement)) and that the interface is meant to be shared by the whole system ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/emi/ni-emi-ioctl_emi_get_measurement)). Firefox (the parent process) and Chromium (the browser process, Medium integrity) open the device with `GENERIC_READ` and have no code that raises rights ([gecko-dev](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mozilla/gecko-dev/master/tools/profiler/core/PowerCounters-win.cpp); [Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/components/power_metrics/energy_metrics_provider_win.cc); [Chromium sandbox](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/docs/design/sandbox.md)). The author of CodeCarbon writes that the reading works from a process without raised rights on Windows 11 (Intel Core Ultra 7 265H) ([PR #1263](https://github.com/mlco2/codecarbon/pull/1263)). This is the author's claim. The CodeCarbon and JoularCore documentation also describe EMI as a path without an administrator ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/methodology/); [JoularCore](https://joular.github.io/joularcore/guide/supported_platforms.html)). The default device descriptor in WDM gives everyone read access ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/kernel/controlling-device-access)), so the result fits. But the driver sets the descriptor, nobody has published it, and we found no report of `ERROR_ACCESS_DENIED`. Both browsers do not record the error code, so the lack of reports is weak evidence. **Conclusion: very likely, not confirmed.** We did not check this on our own Windows 11 hardware.

Availability depends on the Windows version, not on rights. Mozilla wrote on 17 June 2022 that on Windows 11 EMI returns RAPL from the processor, and on Windows 10 only on machines with hardware meters, for example the Surface Book 1 ([Bugzilla 1774844](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1774844)). The FOSDEM 2024 slides say the same. They add Intel (processor, graphics, DRAM) and AMD Ryzen from Windows 11 22H2 (processor, one channel per core) ([FOSDEM 2024](https://archive.fosdem.org/2024/events/attachments/fosdem-2024-2716-firefox-power-profiling-a-powerful-visualization-of-web-sustainability/slides/22726/FOSDEM_2024_-_Firefox_Power_Profiling_a_powerfu_Q635bzv.pdf)). The first AMD reading is a Ryzen 7 5800U with the channels `RAPL_Package0_PKG` and `RAPL_Package0_Core{N}_CORE` ([Bugzilla 1797697](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1797697)). Four independent code bases (Firefox, Chromium, CodeCarbon, tasksmack) read EMI on Windows 11 without their own driver ([tasksmack PR #2873](https://github.com/mgradwohl/tasksmack/pull/2873)). No source gives the oldest Windows 11 build with this feature or a list of processor generations. There is nothing about Whiskey Lake (our i5-8265U) on Windows 11. Our laptop confirms the negative side on Windows 10 (own measurement, 11 Nov 2026): the "Energy Meter" set exists but has no instances, the EMI interface class is not in the registry, and the `intelppm` and `intelpep` drivers run anyway.

It is not known who provides the EMI device with RAPL data. The only source with a driver name is the description of one pull request: the built-in Microsoft PPM driver would pass the MSR registers to EMI, and the author notes that the results are not independently checked ([green-metrics-tool PR #1801](https://github.com/green-coding-solutions/green-metrics-tool/pull/1801)). The study of the Intel "Energy Meter" driver adds a second hypothesis (research emd-1). A driver exists with the hardware ID `SWC\VID8086_EMD_1.0` (INF `energymeterdriver.inf`, versions 1.4.1001.0 from 9 June 2025 and 1.4.1002.0 and 1.4.1003.0 from 25 January 2026) ([Treexy](https://treexy.com/products/driver-fusion/database/system/intel/energy-meter/)). The Microsoft Update Catalog shows it as "Intel Corporation System Driver Update" only for Windows 11 22H2, 24H2 and 25H2 ([Microsoft Update Catalog](https://www.catalog.update.microsoft.com/Search.aspx?q=VID8086_EMD_1.0)). The prefix `SWC\` means a software component created by the INF of an extension of another device (`AddComponent`) ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/install/inf-addcomponent-directive)). The parent device was not found. No source links this driver to EMI or RAPL, and it is not in the HP packages (IPF, DTT, PMT). Conclusion of emd-1: on Windows 10 with an 8th generation processor there will probably be no counters, and the answer remains unknown, leaning toward "no". If this component is what creates EMI, then the presence of EMI depends on whether Windows Update delivered the driver. So on computers managed by the university it may differ from machine to machine (own conclusion). This is why the client must check availability on each computer separately.

When the device exists, the channels are named `RAPL_Package0_PKG` (package), `RAPL_Package0_PP0` (cores), `RAPL_Package0_PP1` (integrated graphics) and `RAPL_Package0_DRAM`. On AMD there are also `RAPL_Package0_Core{N}_CORE`. PKG contains the cores and the graphics, so a sum of the channels counts twice. CodeCarbon takes only the package channels ([windows_emi.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlco2/codecarbon/master/codecarbon/core/windows_emi.py)). The DRAM channel can be present and always equal to 0 ([green-metrics-tool PR #1801](https://github.com/green-coding-solutions/green-metrics-tool/pull/1801)). Chromium also knows four AMD-style names (`VDDCR_VDD Energy`, `VDDCR_SOC Energy`, `Current Socket Energy`, `Apu Energy`), and its authors write that they do not know exactly what they measure ([energy_metrics_provider.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/components/power_metrics/energy_metrics_provider.h)). Neither Firefox nor Chromium maps the PSYS channel (the whole platform) on Windows, so its presence is not confirmed. It is worth looking for, because on three Linux laptops PSYS showed the same as an external watt meter, that is the whole laptop, although without error figures ([Raffin and Trystram](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)). CodeCarbon warns about an inflated PSYS on older Intel processors ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)). CodeCarbon documents EMI only on hardware without virtualization ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/methodology/)). For virtual machines and for Arm there are no tests.

## Apart from EMI and the battery, no path works without an administrator or someone else's driver

The same data stream is exposed as the "Energy Meter" counter set (`Power`, `Energy`, `Time`). Two small projects read it through PDH as `\Energy Meter(RAPL_Package0_PKG)\Power` and report the power in milliwatts ([Power-Sentinel](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/oucdc/Power-Sentinel/main/notebook_power_monitor/rapl.py); [den-watcher PR #91](https://github.com/Programmer-Den/den-watcher/pull/91)). These are the only sources of the unit, and the author of the second project did not test it on real hardware. Microsoft does not document this set or its provider. It does say that PDH reads V2 sets natively, and that the registry interface under .NET `PerformanceCounter` reads them only through a translation layer and is not recommended in new code ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/perfctrs/about-performance-counters)). The .NET code has no support for PDH or V2 ([PerformanceCounterLib.cs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dotnet/runtime/main/src/libraries/System.Diagnostics.PerformanceCounter/src/System/Diagnostics/PerformanceCounterLib.cs)). Our probe through .NET gave zero values on Windows 10, which says nothing about Windows 11. The "Power Meter" (ACPI) set has instances on our laptop, but its counters show 0 (own measurement, 11 Nov 2026).

The only processor power in watts that we saw on Windows 10 comes from the WMI class `root\wmi` `EsifDeviceInformation` of Intel Dynamic Tuning (DTT, a driver from HP). An ordinary user gets "Access denied". As an administrator you see tenths of a watt in the `Power` field: participant 3 (most likely the processor package) shows 1-4.7 W at idle and 23-27 W under full load of eight threads, participant 4 (most likely the cores) a little less, and the temperatures are 45-77°C (own measurement, 11 Nov 2026). We inferred the unit and the meaning of the participants from this test, because Intel does not document the class. The denial agrees with the default Microsoft policy: every WMI GUID has a descriptor, which since Windows Server 2003 lets in only administrators by default, and the driver INF can change this ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/install/inf-ddinstall-wmi-section)). Another project reports the same "Access denied" without an administrator ([ccdeck PR #751](https://github.com/BarganConstantin/ccdeck/pull/751)). Whether this is exactly that mechanism is not confirmed, because the error code was not recorded. In addition, DTT is delivered by the computer maker, and the user cannot install it alone (search engine summary) ([Intel](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/support/articles/000102775.html)). The 2026 releases are only for Windows 11 ([DTT release notes](https://downloadmirror.intel.com/927220/Intel_DTT_PPP_Release_Notes_9.1.10011.2963.pdf)), and the `intel/dptf` repository was archived on 31 Aug 2026 ([GitHub](https://github.com/intel/dptf)).

## The battery measures the whole laptop, but only when it is not on the charger

A laptop has the only meter for the whole computer that an ordinary program can see. On the HP 250 G7 three interfaces gave the same charge in mWh without an administrator: WMI `root\wmi` `BatteryStatus`, WinRT `Windows.Devices.Power.Battery` and `IOCTL_BATTERY_QUERY_STATUS` (own measurement, 11 Nov 2026). The discharge rate is useless. On the charger `DischargeRate` has the value -2147483648 (`BATTERY_UNKNOWN_RATE`), and `ChargeRate` is 0. Microsoft allows such a result: the `Rate` field can be unknown ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/Power/battery-status-str)), and the WinRT properties are empty when the battery controller does not provide them ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/Windows.Devices.Power.BatteryReport)). So you must compute the charge difference between readings.

The night of 10-11 November shows what comes out of this. The laptop ran on the battery from 18:31 to 08:50 (about 14.3 h) and nobody used it. `powercfg /batteryreport` (without an administrator) gave **32,684 mWh**, an average of **2.28 W**, and 2.16-2.53 W in single hours (own measurement). `powercfg /srumutil` (administrator) shows the Windows model, the Energy Estimation Engine (E3). For the same night it gave 33.27 Wh, within 2% of the battery. But **23.5 Wh of this (71%, own calculation) is "EnergyLoss"**: E3 fills up its model to the measured battery discharge. On the charger `EnergyLoss` is always 0 and E3 is only a model. It then gave about 1.6 W on average over 18 h of mostly idle time (the real idle on the battery: 2.3 W) and about 3.2 W during 2.3 h of work with the screen on, which is an underestimate (own measurement). Microsoft itself notes that E3 does not have to reflect the energy from the wall socket ([Microsoft Sustainable Software](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/sustainable-software/measuring-your-application-power-and-carbon-impact-part-1/)). So E3 is not an independent source, it needs an administrator and it does not suit the client. Its only use is to confirm that the battery is the right anchor of the measurement.

We know the quality of the meter mainly from requirements. Windows requires an ACPI battery to have accuracy of 95% or better and capacity granularity of up to 1%, in the upper range about 0.25%. It leaves the sampling and averaging times to the maker ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/acpi-battery-and-power-subsystem-firmware-implementation)). This is a claim, not a measurement. The only characterisation with a DAQ recorder concerns a ThinkPad T61 from 2010: the readings were filtered with a delay of about 16 s, and the RMS error was 19% ([Sesame](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.2831)). We found no independent study of a modern laptop. A preliminary log from our laptop (`energy-log.csv`, 11 Nov 2026, 16:01-16:18; this is not a controlled test) shows `RemainingCapacity` changing by 22-228 mWh at intervals from a dozen seconds to 2 minutes. On the charger the value stayed constant for 19 minutes at 35,864 mWh, and the first reading after unplugging the adapter jumped by +228 mWh. The YouTrack task draft (`youtrack-draft-battery-energy.md`), however, speaks of a step of about 2,177 mWh (6%). We did not explain this difference. The draft also notes a jump to 17.5 W in the last minutes before the discharge, which is not consumption.

For reports every 300 s one thing follows. At 2.3 W one interval is about 190 mWh (own calculation), which is 1-2 updates of the meter. A single report therefore has a large relative error, but the sums from an unbroken chain of readings are correct, because the quantization errors cancel out. The battery measures the DC side, so it does not include the losses of the power adapter. On the power adapter there is no measurement of the whole laptop at all: the USB-C PD contract stays in the driver, and Windows shows only its own notification about slow charging ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/usbcon/usb-type-c-slow-charging-notification-requirements)). This applies also to all desktops, which have no battery.

## The package is 12-86% of the power draw, so the reading must be calibrated

The processor package is only a part of the computer's power draw. The CodeCarbon documentation (Linux, two laptops) says that the package is 58% of the platform power at idle and 86% under load on the Core Ultra 7 265H, but only 12% and 52% on the Core i7-7600U. On a 2017 HEDT desktop it is 39% at idle and 62% under load ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/rapl/)). On servers the RAPL measurements covered 53-66% of the energy ([Brunnert, 2026](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1526482/contributions/7027733/attachments/3268614/5838422/2026-05-06_SC4RC_How_accurate_are_current_tools_and_models_for_software_energy_estimation.pdf)). Office computers spend most of the time at idle, which is where RAPL is weakest. On Alder Lake RAPL stayed at about 1.5 W while the draw from the wall socket changed ([Schöne et al., ICPE 2024](https://research.spec.org/icpe_proceedings/2024/proceedings/p95.pdf)). RAPL on AMD is a model, not a measurement, and the package is significantly lower than the draw from the wall socket ([Schöne et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00808)). In slides from 2026 RAPL (Intel and AMD) was 5-43% lower than IPMI on servers ([HEPiX 2026](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1708318/contributions/7185021/attachments/3324707/5953607/RAPL_HEPiX_Aug2026_v2.pdf)). Intel can add noise to RAPL (about 2% for a 1 s window and 5-15% for 100 ms) ([Intel](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/software-security-guidance/advisory-guidance/running-average-power-limit-energy-reporting.html)). For a 300 s window this does not matter.

The best supported scaling method is a linear fit separately for each model: draw from the wall socket ≈ idle constant of the model + coefficient × (package power − package power at idle). The coefficient came out at about 1.17-1.18 ([Jay et al., 2023](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CCGRID2023_Ostapenco_Jay_Lefevre.pdf)), and the error was 1.7-4.3% (MAPE) on cluster nodes ([Khan et al., 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content)). For the package on laptops and for AMD processors there is no published validation with an error figure, so a validation sample for each model family is mandatory. EMI has not been compared with an external meter ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/accuracy/)). One author compared EMI with an MSR driver and saw a difference of 0.1-0.7% ([green-metrics-tool PR #1801](https://github.com/green-coding-solutions/green-metrics-tool/pull/1801)). This proves only that EMI passes RAPL faithfully, not that RAPL agrees with the wall socket (own conclusion). The substitute "load × TDP" differs from RAPL by from about -60% to about +190% ([CodeCarbon](https://docs.codecarbon.io/latest/explanation/accuracy/)).

Our `energy_rates` table (laptop 15/9/3 W, desktop 55/40/25 W for Active/Idle/Unused) has the same problem. For the night on the battery it would give about 50 Wh (10 min Active × 15 W, 50 min Idle × 9 W, 13.3 h Unused × 3 W) against 32.7 Wh measured. This is one laptop and one night, so a signal, not proof (own measurement). External data also make us suspect 9 W at Idle for a laptop. ENERGY STAR gives "short idle" of 4.8 W and 4.1 W for the ThinkPad T14 and T14s Gen 6 ([T14](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4021616); [T14s](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4016503)), and Notebookcheck measured an average of 4.6 W for the T14s Gen 3 ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Lenovo-ThinkPad-T14s-G3-Intel-reviewed-quiet-mobile-companion-with-great-performance.676256.0.html)). For desktops, 40 W (Idle) and 25 W (Unused) lie higher than the declarations of new HP computers: the EliteDesk 8 Mini G1i has 10.7 W "short idle" and 4.4 W "long idle" ([Mini](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/3999662)), and the Tower G1i 22.6 W and 3.7 W ([Tower](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-computers/details/4005857)). The university fleet may be older, and the columns in this source were read from PDFs, so the mapping is uncertain. A preliminary log `energy-log.csv` (16:03-16:17, 14 minutes on the battery, load and screen brightness unknown) gives an order of magnitude for our laptop: the average "package" power from DTT 2.3 W at a battery draw of about 5.1 W (1,197 mWh in 851 s), so the package is about 46% (own calculation). This fits in the range from the literature, but it is one manual trial.

A true measurement from the wall socket can come only from an external meter, and cheap plugs are weak exactly at the loads of computers. Tasmota documents wrong readings below about 5 W in popular chips and advises against calibration with computer hardware ([Tasmota](https://tasmota.github.io/docs/Power-Monitoring-Calibration)). The Shelly Plus Plug S showed nothing below 2 W in one test (search engine summary) ([home&smart](https://www.homeandsmart.de/shelly-plug-s-test)). The only peer-reviewed study (2023) found an energy error of 54-100% for four of five brands at fast changing power electronics loads, and a PC power adapter is such a load ([e-Prime 2023](https://doaj.org/article/d4bb2be5645c4a28895ab01e491f4b5b)). So every plug model must be checked with a laboratory meter at about 2, 5, 10, 25 and 50 W. You must compare energy (Wh) from long windows, not instant watts.

## The client should collect data for calibration, not pretend to be a wall socket meter

The contract is clear: `energy_uj` is the energy from the wall socket. Sending it, even as 0, means `energy_source` = `measured` for the server. Without the field the server estimates from `energy_rates`, and on the battery it writes 0 (`docs/design/api-contract.md`, `shared/README.md`). No reading available to an ordinary program is the energy from the wall socket: the processor package is a part of the draw, and the battery is the DC side. Writing them into `energy_uj` would be silently false, because cost and CO2 would be computed from an underestimate and labelled as measured. So `energy_uj` stays unchanged, and the measured readings go in separate fields. The table shows the split. The names of the new fields are proposals to be agreed, and adding a field is a separate pull request on both sides, because `shared/README.md` requires this.

| Report field | Source | Send when | Omit (do not send 0) when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `energy_uj` | no reading: there is no wall socket measurement | never from RAPL or from the battery | always; on the battery the server writes 0, off the battery it estimates |
| `battery_energy_uj` | battery charge difference × 3,600,000 µJ/mWh | laptop with a battery, absolute units, unbroken chain of readings | first report, change of power source, no battery, unknown value (0xFFFFFFFF), relative units |
| `device_energy_uj` | −`battery_energy_uj` | only on the battery and when the result is ≥ 0 | on the power adapter |
| new: CPU package energy (name to be agreed) | EMI, channel `RAPL_Package0_PKG`; µJ = pWh × 0.0036 | Windows 11, EMI device open, increments ≥ 0 | no device, access error, counter regression, gap in readings |
| new: source marker (name to be agreed) | what worked in the interval: `emi`, `battery`, `none` | always | never |

The current client fits these readings well. The loop in `Worker.cs` has a 5 s step, and the CPU accumulator has a protocol: a reference point, a gap invalidates the interval, a counter regression is `Invalid` (`Monitoring/CPU-intervals.md`). EMI energy must be treated the same way, because it is an accumulated counter like `GetSystemTimes`. Reading every 5 s does not spoil the measurement: the effect of frequent RAPL polling is visible on an Intel server only from about 10 Hz ([Raffin and Trystram](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15985)). Only positive increments are summed, and samples with the same timestamp are skipped, as Firefox does ([gecko-dev](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mozilla/gecko-dev/master/tools/profiler/core/PowerCounters-win.cpp)). The raw RAPL counter in Haswell and Skylake has 32 bits and a unit of 61 µJ, so it wraps around every 4.9 h at 15 W and every 17 min at 250 W ([Khan et al., 2018](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/bdc6c9a5-74d4-494b-ae83-860625a665ce/content); own calculation). It is not known how EMI behaves at a wrap-around and after sleep, so an interval with a suspicious difference is sent without the field, not with zero. SetupAPI calls (`SetupDiGetClassDevs` and related), `CreateFile` and `DeviceIoControl` through P/Invoke are enough, without a new package, just as `GetSystemPowerStatus` is used today in `WindowsPowerMonitor`. The IOCTL codes are `0x224000`, `0x224004`, `0x224008` and `0x22400C` ([windows_emi.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlco2/codecarbon/master/codecarbon/core/windows_emi.py)). The device search must be done outside the main thread, because the enumeration can hang on a slow ACPI call ([tasksmack PR #2873](https://github.com/mgradwohl/tasksmack/pull/2873)). The handle is opened once with `GENERIC_READ`. The client goes through all interfaces (Chromium takes the first one and has a question in its code whether this is always the right one), chooses the channel `RAPL_Package0_PKG` and does not sum channels. It treats every failure, also code 5 (`ERROR_ACCESS_DENIED`), as "unknown" and records it once. It never asks for an administrator.

For the battery, `IOCTL_BATTERY_QUERY_STATUS` through P/Invoke fits the project: no new package, just like `GetSystemPowerStatus`. WMI is the least code, but it needs the `System.Management` package (this is how the EnergyReader prototype was built), and a new dependency is allowed only when the task asks for it (`AGENTS.md`). WinRT needs a change of `TargetFramework` in .NET to a variant with a Windows SDK version (general knowledge, not from the notes). All three interfaces gave the same result on our laptop (own measurement). The rules from the task draft stay: the field is omitted at the first report, at a change of power source, with relative units and with an unknown value.

What the client should not do now. Do not rely on `EsifDeviceInformation`: the class needs an administrator, it is undocumented, the unit was derived from one test, and the values are momentary (in the log every 2 s the package power jumped from 0.3 to 22 W), so you would have to poll every 1-2 s. A helper service as LocalSystem would solve the access (this is how Intel's `esifsvc` works, own measurement), but it means an installation with an administrator and our own code with high rights. The cost is out of proportion to a pilot. Changing the WMI descriptor by an administrator is a change of security and is not recommended. Do not use .NET `PerformanceCounter` for "Energy Meter" until the probe on Windows 11 shows instances. For now do not add third-party drivers or the AMD Ryzen Master SDK.

In the end the measured data serve to improve `energy_rates` separately for each model, not to show the "true" energy. For laptops: the median of the measured watts per state (Active, Idle, Unused) from reports on the battery. On Windows 11 laptops, both sources together will let us learn the package-to-whole relation on the battery and apply it on the power adapter, without the adapter losses (own conclusion, to be validated with a plug). There are two reservations. The delay of the battery meter (up to 2 minutes) blurs the energy between neighbouring intervals, so you must calibrate on series of intervals in the same state. Calibration per model needs a model identifier that the contract does not have: `machine_hash` is a hash, so it cannot be read back. The server stores `battery_energy_uj` and `device_energy_uj`, but the history API does not return them yet (`docs/design/api-contract.md`).

There are four decisions for Darek that the report does not settle. Whether to add a field with the CPU package energy and a source marker to the contract. Whether to accept that on desktops and on Windows 10 the client will report only the model for a long time. Whether to show the measured values in the tray app (the task draft says: yes). Whether to ask for a model identifier for calibration (privacy and the contract).

## Conclusion

The report changes the question. Instead of "how to read the true energy of a computer", we must ask which computers will be the reference machines and how to check them with a plug. Most of the fleet, that is desktops and Windows 10, will stay with the model for a long time. So the client is a calibration sensor, not a meter. The value of the measured fields lies in improving the table separately for each model, not in replacing it.

Two results are not obvious. First, Microsoft's own numbers on the battery (E3) are 71% a fill-up to the battery reading. So a laptop on the battery is an unusually clean reference point, while the same laptop on the power adapter is already a model. Results from the battery must not be moved to the power adapter without a reference from a plug, because the battery does not see the adapter losses. Second, next to access rights, availability is just as important: whether Windows 11 on the university computers has the EMI device at all may depend on the delivery of a driver. Availability will be settled by a probe on Windows 11, and the relation to the wall socket by a test with a plug. Both tests are cheap and will give an answer before any client code is written.

## What is still not confirmed

- Whether an ordinary user opens EMI on Windows 11. The evidence is indirect (three programs, one claim), the device descriptor is not published, and we did not test it. Restricted tokens and services on other accounts were also not checked.
- Who provides the EMI device with RAPL (the PPM driver, the Intel component `SWC\VID8086_EMD_1.0` or something else) and whether its presence depends on Windows Update. The parent device for `EMD` is unknown, and the data about it come from an aggregator and a catalog (research emd-1).
- From which Windows 11 build EMI with RAPL works. There is no list of processor generations, nothing about Whiskey Lake or others before Ice Lake, and for AMD we know only Zen 3 (5800U) and the names in the Chromium code.
- Whether the PSYS channel (the whole platform) appears through EMI on Windows.
- The "Energy Meter" set: provider, access rights, behaviour of .NET `PerformanceCounter`, the unit (milliwatts from one project) and instances on Windows 11.
- The behaviour of EMI counters at a wrap-around and after sleep or hibernation. Microsoft states that the accumulation depends on the query frequency and on the driver.
- The accuracy of EMI against an external meter, and the dependence of RAPL on the draw from the wall socket on laptops and on client AMD. The only comparison of EMI with MSR comes from one author.
- The class `EsifDeviceInformation`: the unit and the meaning of the participants come from one test on one laptop, the mechanism of the access denial is unconfirmed, and the behaviour on Lenovo, Dell and on Windows 11 is unknown.
- The resolution and accuracy of the battery meter: steps of 22-228 mWh in the log of 11 Nov 2026 versus a step of about 2,177 mWh in the task draft. There is no independent study of a modern laptop, and other models may report the discharge rate.
- The package-to-whole relation on our laptop: only one manual trial of 14 minutes (about 46%), without a wall socket measurement.
- The correctness of `energy_rates` for desktops: no measurement at all. The ENERGY STAR values are declarations of makers, and the definitions of "short idle" and "long idle" and the column mapping were not checked.
- The AMD Ryzen Master Monitoring SDK (administrator requirement, driver, list of processors, licence text) and Intel PMT (device ACL, whether it contains package energy). The maker pages did not answer, and the data come from search engine summaries.
- Virtual machines, VDI, VBS/HVCI and Arm: no tests.
- The makeup of the university fleet: Windows versions, processors, desktops versus laptops, driver and Defender policies.
- The details of the Firefox, Chromium and CodeCarbon code come from summaries of the fetching tool, not from the raw files. Before writing code the raw sources must be checked.
- The accuracy of cheap plugs at small loads: there is no controlled test, and the numbers of Tasmota, Shelly and e-Prime come from documentation, a search engine summary and an article summary.
